The three films, honestly

How it worksDye layer absorbs lightCarbon particles block light & some infraredCeramic nanoparticles block light, IR, and UV

Prices are typical installed ranges for a full sedan (five windows, no windshield) in 2026 — steep windshields, Teslas, and full-glass jobs run more. A quote far below these ranges is usually a dyed film sold with a ceramic-sounding name.

Who should buy which

  • Dyed: you want the look, the car won't be yours in three years, budget is the whole story.
  • Carbon: the value pick — real heat rejection, no fading, mid price. The right answer for most daily drivers.
  • Ceramic: you park outside, commute in traffic, have kids or leather seats, or want max heat/UV cut without going dark. One summer usually settles the debate.

Is ceramic worth it?

Run the math on years, not dollars. A $550 ceramic install on a car you'll keep for six years is about $92 a year for a cooler cabin, 99% UV cut (your dash and seats age slower, so does your left arm), and a film that still looks new when you sell. The $200 dyed job that purples in year three costs more per good year than it looks.

Three questions that expose a bad shop

  1. "Which film brand and line, exactly?" — a real shop names it without blinking.
  2. "What does the warranty cover, and who backs it?" — film maker vs. "the shop's word" are different things.
  3. "Is this shade legal on my car in this state?" — the right answer is a number, not "everybody runs it."
